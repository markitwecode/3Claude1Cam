[c1.board.1] {under} A breadboard hides metal strips under its plastic. {rows} The long rows along the top and bottom are joined end to end, and they carry power. {columns} In the middle, the holes are joined in short columns of five. {joined} Anything you push into a column is connected to everything else in it.

[c1.board.2] {trench} Along the middle runs a trench, and no column crosses it. {chip} A chip sits astride the trench, so each pin gets its own column, {apart} and the pins facing each other across the trench stay apart.

[c1.board.3] {strip} Power comes from the white strip beside the boards. Its left column is zero volts, its right column five volts. {jumpers} Short jumper wires carry both to the board's rows, and every chip needs both. {missing} Some rows start with no jumpers, so add them. {pulled} Someone may have pulled some out, so check first. {split} And a long row can be broken halfway, leaving a chip on the far half unpowered.

[c1.board.4] {crowd} Give yourself room. A leg pushed into the wrong column makes a connection you never meant. {prizes} As the lecturer says, there's no prize for making it as small as possible. {short} But keep the wires short. It's neater, you make fewer mistakes, and faults are easier to find. {flow} And lay it out left to right, from inputs to outputs.

[c1.chips.1] {package} Each chip, or integrated circuit, comes in a dual in-line package. {pins} This one has fourteen pins, seven on each side. {sixteen} The lab's counter chip has sixteen.

[c1.chips.2] {find} Find pin one first. Older chips have a white dot there, but newer laser markings are hard to read, {notch} so look for the semicircular notch at one end. {wrongway} Get it wrong, and the whole chip is wired wrong. {count} Seen from above, the pins count anticlockwise, one to seven along the bottom, then eight to fourteen back along the top.

[c1.chips.3] {power} Pin seven is ground, and pin fourteen is five volts. {corners} They sit at opposite corners. {sixteen2} On a sixteen-pin chip, it's eight and sixteen. {turn} Give it a quarter turn, as the handout draws it, and you get the lecturer's rule. Bottom left is ground, top right is five volts. {exceptions} There are exceptions, but none among the lab's chips.

[c1.chips.4] {part} Now the part number, seventy-four H C zero zero. {series} Seventy-four is the series, around since the nineteen-sixties. {tech} H C is the technology, high-speed see-moss, which replaced the older bipolar chips, as those were too power-hungry and expensive. See-moss is in nearly every chip today, and the course comes back to it later. {func} Zero zero is the function, four two-input NAND gates, a quad NAND.

[c1.chips.5] {never} Never take a pin-out for granted. {pair} Here's the seventy-four zero zero, a quad NAND, beside the seventy-four zero two, a quad NOR. {same} Same package, same power pins. {outs} But the NAND's outputs are pins three, six, eight and eleven, and the NOR's are one, four, ten and thirteen. {booklet} The lab has a pin-out booklet. Use it.

Others have used them before you. {insert} Second, insert all the chips the same way round, with no pin folded under or sideways. {power} Third, wire five volts and zero volts to every chip. {tie} Fourth, tie down the unused inputs. {rest} Then the rest.

[c1.build.2] {why} Why power first? Diagrams usually leave out the supply pins, so they're easy to forget. {backdoor} And a see-moss chip with no supply can power itself up through any input that's at five volts. {half} Then it half works, depending on the inputs, almost working, but not quite.

[c1.build.3] {spare} Workshop one starts with one gate in use on a chip, and three spare. {pickup} A see-moss input has a very high resistance, so tiny currents from stray electromagnetic radiation become real voltages. {flicker} A floating input drifts, and its gate switches at random. {meter} See-moss power goes with how often it switches, so the chip draws more power, {upset} and the other gates on it can misbehave too.

[c1.build.4] {story} The lecturer tells of a shop whose heating controller switched things on and off whenever someone waved a hand near it. {inside} Inside, unused inputs had been left unconnected. {fix} So tie every unused gate input to zero volts. {control} On bigger chips, a control input goes to whichever level keeps it idle, so check the pin-out.

[c1.build.5] {outputs} But never tie an unused output. An output either drives inputs, or it's left alone. {short} Tied to ground, it tries to push five volts into a short circuit whenever it outputs a one. {plant} And remember, power goes with switching. It's back in part two.

[c1.faults.1] {scope} When a circuit fails, measure it. An oscilloscope plots voltage against time, but it takes desk space, so it's for when you're really stuck. {probe} If you use one, clip its probe's ground to the circuit's ground. {dmm} In the lab, you'll mostly use a digital multimeter, as a voltmeter.

[c1.faults.2] {hunt} Then hunt in order. {supply} First, the supply pins. Are five volts and zero volts where you expect? {short} If every five volt pin reads zero, suspect a short between the supplies. {clock} Second, the clock signal. Is it reaching every chip that needs it, at the rate you set? It's easy to miss a flip-flop. {follow} Third, follow the signal. Check a gate's inputs, {output} then its output against what it should be, {nextpin} then that output arriving at the next input pin. {stop} Stop at the first thing that looks fishy.

[c1.faults.3] {fishy} In a logic circuit, every reading should be near zero or five volts. So one point two volts, say, should never appear. {fight} Usually, two outputs are wired together and fighting, one pulling to one, and the other to zero. {broken} Sometimes, the chip is broken. {bin} Bin it, never back in the box, or it's a booby trap for next week's class. {ask} Still stuck?
