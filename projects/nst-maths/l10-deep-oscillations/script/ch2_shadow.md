[c2.guess.1] {guess} You may already have a guess from the wave's shape. Hold on to it. {hard} The two derivatives are what make the rule hard. So look for a function that differentiating barely changes. {grow} E to the T is such a function. Its rate of change always equals its value. {decay} E to the minus T decays, and its rate of change is minus its value. {knob} For E to the lambda T, the rate of change is lambda times the value. [c2.guess.2] {move} So the move here is: try E to the lambda T, because differentiating only multiplies it by lambda. {d2} Do it twice, and you get lambda squared times E to the lambda T. {sub} Put that into the rule, {cancel} and divide out the exponential, which is never zero. {result} The whole rule shrinks to one line: lambda squared equals minus omega squared.

[c2.guess.3] {think} Say omega is two, so you need lambda squared to be minus four. Which lambda does that? Pause the video if you want longer. {pause=4} {real} No real number works: any real number, squared, is zero or more. That's why the graphs never swing. {imag} But eye squared is minus one, so two eye, all squared, is minus four. In general, eye omega, all squared, is minus omega squared, and minus eye omega works too. {answer} So lambda is plus or minus eye omega.

[c2.guess.4] {ask} So the rule itself asks for complex numbers. But does E to the eye omega T really change at eye omega times its value? You can watch it and see. {tag} One note: this route goes beyond the notes, which simply state the answer. This small tag marks each addition.
