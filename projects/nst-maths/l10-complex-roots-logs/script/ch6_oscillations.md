## Scene 2 · Simple harmonic motion (ch6_s2_pendulum)

> {pendulum}: left half, the pendulum from the lecturer's margin sketch (scan p. 10): hatched ceiling, a DIM dashed vertical, the rod labelled `l`, the bob `m`, a small arrow `g` pointing down. > Running example for s2–s5 (it is s5's answer, revealed there): x(t) = cos ωt + √3 sin ωt = 2 cos(ωt − π/3), i.e. c = 1. Keep the swing small on screen (about ±20°); at t = 0 it sits at half its full angle, swinging outward.

> {formula}: `x(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t` with a DIM `(81)` beside it (the notes' line). {cos} and {sin} underline the two terms in turn. {coeffs}: `a` and `b` turn BLUE (real constants), with small tags "cosine coefficient" and "sine coefficient". {shm}: the notes' boxed term "simple harmonic motion" (YELLOW) under the formula.

> {omega_t}: `\omega t` (YELLOW) with a small counter, "+ ω every second" on {grows}. {repeat}: the graph's first crest and the next one flash. {period}: a TEAL brace over one full cycle, `2\pi/\omega`, "one swing, there and back" (TEAL as in Part 1: one full turn, 2π).
> {fixed}: two DIM curves with other starts (other a, b) lay over the graph: different heights and shifts, and the same TEAL brace fits all three.

[c6.pend.1] {pendulum} Take a pendulum, a weight on a rod of length L, swinging under gravity. {angle} Its position at time T is one number, the angular displacement, ex of T. It's the angle between the rod and the vertical. {omega} For small swings, it rocks back and forth with an angular frequency, omega, equal to the square root of G over L, the strength of gravity divided by the length. {given} We'll take that as given. The physics comes next term.

[c6.pend.2] {formula} Then the displacement always has this form. {cos} It's a real constant times the cosine of omega T, {sin} plus another real constant times the sine of omega T. {coeffs} We'll call them the cosine coefficient and the sine coefficient. {shm} This is called simple harmonic motion. {graph} Plot it against time, and you get a smooth wave.

[c6.pend.3] {omega_t} So what does omega mean? {pause} {grows} The quantity omega T grows by omega every second. {repeat} Cosine and sine repeat each time it grows by two pi. {period} So one full swing, there and back, takes two pi over omega seconds. {fixed} Start the pendulum differently, and the coefficients change, but that time stays the same.

## Scene 3 · The turning arrow and its shadow (ch6_s3_phasor)

> Left: ch1's Argand plane (Re and Im labels, DIM grid) with a DIM circle of radius 2 (the running example's modulus). The pendulum shrinks to a small inset top right and keeps swinging in step with everything below.
> {arrow}: a GOLD arrow from 0, starting at angle −π/3, turning anticlockwise at a steady rate, with Figure 24's small curved arrow `\omega` near its tip. {circle}: its tip leaves a faint GOLD trail round the circle. {shadow}: a DIM dashed line from the tip straight to the real axis, and a BLUE dot there, labelled `x(t)` (the notes' figure has a green dot; ours is BLUE, real parts). {swing}: the BLUE dot slides back and forth in step with the inset pendulum. {trace}: the BLUE dot's path unrolls downward under the plane (time running down), a wave on its side; it then turns a quarter turn and lays itself over s2's graph: the same curve.
> {formula}: right: `x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\left[A\,e^{i\omega t}\right]` with a DIM `(82)`. {amp}: a GOLD copy of the arrow freezes at its starting angle, labelled `A` ("t = 0"), and `A` in the formula turns YELLOW with the notes' boxed term "complex amplitude". {turns}: `e^{i\omega t}` glows GOLD while a YELLOW arc `\omega t` grows from the frozen arrow to the moving one; the moving arrow's length stays fixed. {two_angles}: the inset's small YELLOW `x(t)` arc beside the big growing `\omega t` arc, tags "stays small" and "keeps growing".
> {why}: a YELLOW "why the same?" beside the formula. {split}: below, `A = \alpha + i\beta` (`\alpha` BLUE, `i\beta` PINK) and `e^{i\omega t} = \cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t`. {multiply}: `(\alpha + i\beta)(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t)` expands into `\alpha\cos\omega t + i\alpha\sin\omega t + i\beta\cos\omega t + i^2\beta\sin\omega t`, terms with i PINK. {ii}: `i^2` flashes and becomes `-1`, so the last term turns BLUE: `-\beta\sin\omega t`. {re}: the PINK terms dim away, leaving `\mathrm{Re}\left[A\,e^{i\omega t}\right] = \alpha\cos\omega t - \beta\sin\omega t` (scan p. 10, with the lecturer's c̃, d̃ renamed α, β).
> {match}: s2's `x(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t` slides in above; connector lines join cos to cos and sin to sin. {alpha}: `\alpha = a`. {beta}: `\beta = -b`. {box}: boxed YELLOW `A = a - ib` (the notes' line), `a` BLUE and `-ib` PINK.
> {polar}: back in the plane, the frozen arrow `A` gets its polar labels. {mod}: the radius `|A| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}` (GOLD). {arg}: a YELLOW arc from the real axis to `A`, `\arg A`, tagged "phase". {cosform}: `x(t) = |A|\cos(\omega t + \arg A)`, with `\omega t + \arg A` linked by colour to the moving arrow's full angle arc (YELLOW). {fullturn}: the moving arrow completes a turn, a TEAL arc `2\pi` round the circle, linked to s2's TEAL brace `2\pi/\omega`.
> Checked with sympy 1.14.0 (Python 3.11): Re[(α + iβ)(cos ωt + i sin ωt)] = α cos ωt − β sin ωt; Re[(a − ib)e^{iωt}] = a cos ωt + b sin ωt; and |A| cos(ωt + arg A) = a cos ωt + b sin ωt (cmath, three (a, b) pairs).

[c6.arrow.1] {plane} Here's the trick, from Figure twenty-four in the notes. {arrow} Put an arrow in the Argand diagram, and let it turn about zero at a steady rate, omega radians per second. {circle} Its tip runs round a circle. {shadow} Now watch its shadow on the real axis. {swing} The shadow slides back and forth, slowest at the ends and fastest in the middle, just like the pendulum. {trace} Trace it out over time, and you get the same wave as before.

[c6.arrow.2] {formula} In symbols, the displacement is the real part of this turning arrow. {amp} The arrow starts as a fixed complex number, which the notes call the complex amplitude. {turns} Then it's multiplied by E to the eye omega T. Multiplying adds angles, so the arrow keeps its length, and its angle grows by omega T. {two_angles} Careful, that's a different angle from the pendulum's. The pendulum's angle stays small, while the arrow's angle keeps growing.

[c6.arrow.3] {why} But why does this give the same motion? {pause} {split} Write the complex amplitude as alpha plus eye beta, and E to the eye omega T as cosine plus eye sine. {multiply} Multiply out. {ii} Eye times eye is minus one, so the two imaginary pieces multiply to give a real term, minus beta times the sine of omega T. {re} So the real part is alpha times the cosine of omega T, minus beta times the sine of omega T.

[c6.arrow.4] {match} Now compare with the pendulum's formula. {alpha} Alpha is the cosine coefficient. {beta} And beta is minus the sine coefficient. {box} So the complex amplitude is the cosine coefficient, minus eye times the sine coefficient. {pause} Two real constants, packed into one complex number.

[c6.arrow.5] {polar} In polar form, that one number tells you the whole motion. {mod} Its modulus is the radius of the circle, so it's the size of the swing. By Pythagoras, it's the square root of the two coefficients squared and added. {arg} Its argument is the angle where the arrow starts, called the phase. {cosform} So the displacement is the modulus, times the cosine of the arrow's angle, which is omega T plus the phase. {fullturn} And each full turn of the arrow, two pi, is one full swing of the pendulum.

## Scene 4 · Differentiating means multiplying by iω (ch6_s4_derivative)

> s3's plane, circle and GOLD arrow stay on the left, still turning (running example); the right side clears; the pendulum inset stays.
> {v}: right: `v(t) = \dfrac{dx}{dt} = \dfrac{d}{dt}\,\mathrm{Re}\left[A\,e^{i\omega t}\right]`. {swap}: `\mathrm{Re}` and `\dfrac{d}{dt}` trade places, `= \mathrm{Re}\left[\dfrac{d}{dt}\,A\,e^{i\omega t}\right]`; a small aside shows the arrow's BLUE real part and PINK imaginary part, each with its own `d/dt`. {rule}: `= \mathrm{Re}\left[i\omega A\,e^{i\omega t}\right]` with a DIM `(83)`, `i\omega` glowing (`i` PINK). {notes}: under it, the notes' sentence in a YELLOW box: "to differentiate e^{iωt} we simply multiply by iω before taking the real part".
> {check}: below, `\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t\right)`; {diff} `= -\omega\sin\omega t + i\omega\cos\omega t`; {factor} `= i\omega\left(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t\right)`, the `i\omega` pulled out to the front. {same}: a DIM line beside it, `\dfrac{d}{dt}\,e^{kt} = k\,e^{kt}`.
> {i}: in the plane, a copy of the GOLD arrow turns a quarter turn ahead (right-angle mark, `\times i`); {stretch} it stretches by ω (`\times\omega`) and becomes PURPLE, labelled `i\omega A\,e^{i\omega t}` (PURPLE: no conjugate appears in this chapter). {lead}: both arrows turn together, locked at 90°; the PURPLE arrow's shadow is a PURPLE dot on the real axis, labelled `v(t)`.
> {ends}: freeze with the GOLD arrow along +Re (BLUE shadow at its far end, the inset pendulum at the end of its swing): the PURPLE arrow points straight up, its shadow at 0, tag "still". {bottom}: resume, and freeze a quarter turn later: GOLD straight up (BLUE shadow at 0, pendulum at the bottom), PURPLE pointing left, its shadow furthest out, tag "fastest".
> {twice}: `(i\omega)^2 = -\omega^2` (ours); {back} a third arrow, one more quarter turn on, points straight against the GOLD arrow, labelled "acceleration"; {law} `\ddot{x} = -\omega^2 x`, and the inset pendulum gets a small arrow pulling the bob back towards the vertical.
> Checked with sympy 1.14.0: d/dt Re[(α + iβ)e^{iωt}] = Re[iω(α + iβ)e^{iωt}], and d/dt (cos ωt + i sin ωt) = iω(cos ωt + i sin ωt).

[c6.deriv.1] {v} Here's the big advantage. The velocity is the derivative of the displacement with respect to time. {swap} You can take the real part before or after differentiating, because the derivative acts on the real and imaginary parts separately. {rule} So we differentiate the arrow itself. {notes} In the words of the notes, to differentiate E to the eye omega T, we simply multiply by eye omega, before taking the real part.

[c6.deriv.2] {check} You can check that rule with cosine and sine. {diff} Differentiate cosine of omega T, plus eye sine of omega T, and you get minus omega sine, plus eye omega cosine. {factor} That's exactly eye omega, times cosine plus eye sine. {same} So it's the same rule as for a real exponential.

[c6.deriv.3] {i} Now the picture. Multiplying by eye turns the arrow a quarter turn ahead, {stretch} and multiplying by omega stretches it. {lead} So the velocity arrow always leads the position arrow by ninety degrees, and its shadow is the velocity. {ends} When the pendulum reaches the end of its swing, the position arrow lies along the real axis, so the velocity arrow is vertical, and its shadow is zero. For a moment, the pendulum is still. {bottom} A quarter turn later, the pendulum passes the bottom, and the velocity's shadow is furthest out. That's where it's fastest.

[c6.deriv.4] {twice} Do it twice, and you multiply by eye omega twice, which is minus omega squared. {back} So the acceleration arrow points straight back, against the position. {law} The further out the pendulum is, the harder it's pulled back towards the middle. That's the equation of simple harmonic motion, and the course comes back to it next term.

## Scene 5 · Amplitude and phase from the start (ch6_s5_example)

> {setup}: clean screen. {start}: top, the notes' line `x(0) = c` and `v(0) \equiv \dot{x}(0) = \sqrt{3}\,c\,\omega` (notes p. 28), with `c` and `\sqrt{3}\,c\,\omega` GOLD. {facts}: both facts get a small bracket, "the start". {find}: YELLOW "amplitude?  phase?" beside them.
> {zero}: small DIM `e^{i\omega\cdot 0} = 1`. {xzero}: `x(0) = \mathrm{Re}(A) = c`, the `\mathrm{Re}(A)` lifted from s3's formula. {vzero}: `\dot{x}(0) = \mathrm{Re}(i\omega A) = \sqrt{3}\,c\,\omega`, lifted from s4's `(83)` (the notes' line).
> {write}: `A = c + i\beta` (`c` BLUE, `i\beta` PINK). {mult}: `i\omega A = i\omega c - \omega\beta`. {real}: an underbrace on `-\omega\beta`, "real part". {solve}: `-\omega\beta = \sqrt{3}\,c\,\omega \;\Rightarrow\; \beta = -\sqrt{3}\,c`. {result}: `A = c(1 - i\sqrt{3})`.
> {plane}: right: ch1's Argand plane, the point `A` drawn as a right triangle. {legs}: a BLUE leg `c` along Re, then a PINK leg `\sqrt{3}\,c` downward. {mod}: the GOLD hypotenuse, `|A| = \sqrt{c^2 + 3c^2} = 2c`. {angle}: a YELLOW arc below the axis, `-\pi/3` ("−60°"), with a DIM note "c > 0". The line completes as the notes have it: `A = c(1 - i\sqrt{3}) = 2c\,e^{-i\pi/3}`.
> {answer}: "amplitude `|A| = 2c`, phase `-\pi/3`", then boxed YELLOW `x(t) = 2c\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{1}{3}\pi)` (the notes write the ⅓π this way).
> {go}: the GOLD arrow (length 2c) turns from −π/3. {half}: its BLUE shadow starts at `c`, with a brace "half the amplitude", and moves right; s4's PURPLE velocity arrow starts at +π/6, its shadow at `\sqrt{3}\,c\,\omega` (a GREEN tick). {same}: s2's pendulum returns at full size beside it, swinging in step: the running example all along.
> {compare}: a DIM side panel, "sines and cosines": `a = c`, `b = \sqrt{3}\,c`, then `c\cos\omega t + \sqrt{3}\,c\sin\omega t = 2c\cos(\omega t - \pi/3)` with "trig identity" over the arrow, against "complex amplitude": the two boxed lines. {two}: the complex side glows.
> Checked with sympy 1.14.0 and cmath (Python 3.11): Re(A) = c, Re(iωA) = √3cω, β = −√3c, |A| = 2c, arg A = −π/3, 2c e^{−iπ/3} = c(1 − i√3); x(t) = 2c cos(ωt − π/3) = c cos ωt + √3c sin ωt with x(0) = c and v(0) = √3cω; at t = 0 the velocity arrow is iωA = √3cω + icω (modulus 2cω, argument π/6).

[c6.ex.1] {setup} Let's put all this to work on the example in the notes. {start} At time zero, the pendulum is at displacement C, and it's moving with velocity root three times C omega. {facts} Those are two facts about the start. {find} From them, what are the amplitude and the phase of the swing? {pause=1.5}

[c6.ex.2] {zero} At time zero, E to the eye omega T is just one, so the arrows are at their starting positions. {xzero} The starting displacement is the real part of the complex amplitude, so that real part is C. {vzero} The starting velocity is the real part of eye omega times the complex amplitude, so that's root three times C omega.

[c6.ex.3] {write} Since its real part is C, write the complex amplitude as C plus eye beta. {mult} Multiply by eye omega, and you get eye omega C, minus omega beta. {real} The real part is minus omega beta. {solve} Set that equal to root three times C omega, and beta is minus root three times C. {result} So the complex amplitude is C times, one minus eye root three.

[c6.ex.4] {plane} Now draw it. {legs} It's C along the real axis, and root three times C downwards. {mod} By Pythagoras, its length is two C, since one plus three is four. {angle} Its angle is minus sixty degrees, or minus pi over three, taking C to be positive. {answer} So the amplitude is two C, the phase is minus pi over three, and the displacement is two C times the cosine of the arrow's angle, omega T minus pi over three.

[c6.ex.5] {go} Let it run. {half} The shadow starts at C, half the amplitude, and moves outward, with the starting velocity we asked for. {same} In fact, this is the pendulum we've been watching all chapter. {compare} With sines and cosines alone, you'd find both coefficients, then combine them with a trig identity to get the amplitude and phase. {two} The complex amplitude hands you both at once, in two lines.
