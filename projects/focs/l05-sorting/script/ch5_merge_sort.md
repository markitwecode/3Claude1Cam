[c5.merge.1] {two} Here are two lists, each already sorted. Three, five, and four, seven, nine. {how} How can we combine them into one sorted list, quickly? {pause} {heads} The smallest item of all must sit at the front of one list or the other. So we only ever need to compare the two front items. {zip} Take the smaller one, and repeat with what's left.

[c5.merge.2] {code} Here's merge. It takes the two lists together, as a pair. {nil1} If the first list is empty, the answer is the second, just as in append. {nil2} If the second is empty, the answer is the first. {cons} Otherwise, both lists have a head. Call them ex and why. {test} If ex is at most why, {left} ex comes first, followed by the merge of the first list's tail with the whole second list. {right} If not, why comes first, followed by the merge of the whole first list with the second list's tail.

[c5.mcost.1] {placed} So how many comparisons does a merge make? Look at what each one did. Every comparison sent exactly one item to the output. {free} And once one list runs out, the rest of the other follows for free. So at least one item always comes free, the very last. {bound} Merging lists of lengths em and N takes at most em plus N, minus one comparisons. {loose} Elsewhere, the notes say em plus N, a slightly looser bound.

[c5.mcost.2] {low} The exact count depends on how the two lists interleave. Merge one, two, three with four, five, six. The first list runs out after three comparisons, and four, five, six follow free. {high} Now merge one, three, five with two, four, six. The lists alternate to the very end, like the teeth of a zip, so it takes five comparisons, the most possible for six items. {pause}

[c5.mcost.3] {lopsided} Now a lopsided merge, of the list five with one, two, three, four. The five is compared with every item in turn, and lands at the end, after four comparisons. {ins} That's exactly what ins would do. Merge generalises insert to two lists, so when one list has a single item, merging is just insertion. {little} Four comparisons to place one item. Much work, for little gain. {equal} Merging pays off when the two lists have about the same length.

[c5.mcost.4] {deep} One more thing about that trace. {wait} Each step leaves a cons waiting, just like lecture three's append, so merge is not iterative, and its recursion is deep. {acc} An accumulator could make it iterative, but it would still have to build every cell of the answer, and it would build them backwards. {habit} So, just as for the append function, there's little to gain. Never add an accumulator merely out of habit. {pause}

[c5.code.1] {basis} Merging is the basis of several sorting methods. Here's one that divides and conquers, like quicksort. {code} Its name is tee-merge-sort, {name} where the tee stands for top-down. {nil} The empty list is already sorted, {single} and so is a list of one item, the pattern quicksort used. {half} Any longer list gets cut in two. Kay is half its length, rounded down.

[c5.code.2] {left} Sort the first kay items, taken with take. {right} Sort the rest, dropped with drop. {merge} Then merge the two sorted halves. {type} OCaml replies that tee-merge-sort takes an alpha list, and returns an alpha list. {pause} {split} On our list of five, half the length, rounded down, is two. {take} Take gives three, five, {drop} and drop gives nine, four, seven. That's the split last lecture promised. {even} Two items and three. The halves never differ by more than one.

[c5.code.3] {contrast} Compare this with quicksort. Both of them divide, conquer and combine. {quick} Quicksort works hard to divide, comparing every item with the pivot, and then all it does is append the two parts. {mergesort} Merge sort divides by simply counting out half the items, with no comparisons at all. {moves} All its comparisons happen when it combines, inside merge. The work has moved from dividing to combining. {pause} {arrays} The notes add that merge sort is seldom found in conventional programs, because it's hard to code for arrays. {lists} But for lists, as you've just seen, it's natural.
